NASA publishes a resource list for the challenge. Twenty-eight sources: its own satellite missions (SMAP, GPM, Landsat, MODIS, ECOSTRESS, GEDI), most of the USDA catalog (soils, crops, prices, statistics), and partners like Europe's Sentinel-2 and the ERA5 reanalysis.

I sat down and catalogued them all in the project vault. Each source got a card: what it measures, at what resolution, how you get in, and what it's good for in the app. Out of that came a table worth its weight in gold: the same data point usually has three or four providers.

Take soil moisture. Today the app reads it from NASA POWER, a fast API but a modeled one. SMAP actually measures it from space. GLDAS and ERA5 compute it with different models. Four roads to the same number. Same story with rainfall (POWER, IMERG, gauges), pasture vigor (Sentinel-2, Landsat, MODIS) and evapotranspiration (ECOSTRESS, OpenET).

So I designed a source router. The app doesn't ask "which API do I call?" but "what do I need?", and each need has an ordered list: the fastest provider covering that field goes first, and if it fails or stalls, the next one picks up. Some sources answer live, others get preloaded per field because they're too heavy for a demo, and a few exist to defend the numbers in front of the judges.

One wrinkle sorts the whole thing out: half the USDA catalog only covers the United States. For Bolivia the global sources do the work: POWER, Sentinel-2, FAO's GAEZ, ERA5. The USDA stack earns its keep at our US analog site, where official soil maps and year-by-year crop history actually exist.

Crossing sources is what the jury rewards as "Best Use of Data", but it has a more honest benefit: when three independent sources say the same thing about your field, the number defends itself.
