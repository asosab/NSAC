Of the fourteen hackathon challenges, «[Planet X and SPHEREx](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/2026/challenges/planet-x-and-spherex/)» is the one that pulls me most: advanced level, astrophysics, and data from a mission that's flying right now. I spent the afternoon studying it and here's what I found.

![Illustration of the SPHEREx probe above Earth with a cosmic background]({{ '/assets/img/retos/planetax.jpg' | relative_url }})

## What they ask

A public web tool that shows SPHEREx sky images and lets you see how they change between visits. The brief sets five test targets, and they're not random: WISE 0855, Epsilon Indi B and Barnard's star move across the sky; RW Aurigae and R Draconis change brightness. Half movers, half variables. Whatever gets built has to catch both.

## The data

SPHEREx maps the whole sky every six months in 102 near-infrared bands, with six detectors and linear variable filters. The detail that changes everything: each exposure covers a single band per pixel. A point in the sky takes a week or two to complete its 102-channel spectrum. There's no one-day photo with all the colors; each band arrives at a different instant.

The data lives in the IRSA archive: queried through TAP (the `spherex.obscore` table), through the SIA2 protocol, and with URL cutouts over FITS images of several hundred megabytes. It's all quick release, so products change between versions and you have to note which one you used.

## The crosses that can yield rare variables

Here's the interesting part. Detecting "this changed brightness" is something anyone can do; the crosses with other catalogs are what pull out uncommon things:

- **Residual motion.** Propagate the AllWISE or Gaia position (2010–2022) to the epoch of each SPHEREx visit and measure the difference. What no catalog explains is a candidate for a new brown dwarf, an interstellar object or, in the spirit of the challenge, Planet X. An "unexplained displacement" score can rank the viewer.
- **Ephemeris traces.** Ask JPL Horizons where each asteroid and comet should be at the exact instant of the exposure. The mover that falls on the trace is known and gets labeled from the SBDB database; the one that falls on none is a discovery flag.
- **Chromatic variability.** Each detector forms its own light curve. An object that varies at 1.4 µm and stays still at 4 µm is saying something physical, and that's much rarer than an even variation.
- **Spectral variability.** The most exotic variable isn't "it got brighter" but "it changed color": water-ice absorption appearing in a comet, a young disk showing its ice as it dims, dust crossing in front of a Beta Pictoris-type star. You can color the map by "which band varies", an axis almost nobody exploits.
- **Movers that also vary.** The intersection of both lists: rotating asteroids, intermittent comets, binaries.
- **Decade baseline.** AllWISE, NEOWISE and SPHEREx together cover about fifteen years of infrared. Long-period variables only show up by crossing missions.

## What's next

The challenge's official resource list opens on the 13th, a day before the event. The first hour is already decided: a TAP query on WISE 0855, cutouts of each visit, blinking in time order, and checking the motion against AllWISE. If that works, the rest of the weekend is building the viewer.
