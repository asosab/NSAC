End of day one: this is where fieldShift stands, the app for deciding what to plant and when.

The visible front lives at [asosab.github.io/fieldShift](https://asosab.github.io/fieldShift/): a landing page and the app itself, which opens on a satellite basemap (Esri's layer) and lets you draw parcels on top. Getting in takes email and password; signup sends a six-digit code through Gmail, and password recovery uses the same path. I tested it against real mail, not a mock.

Inside, each user has farms and parcels as polygons. If the polygon already exists in a file, nobody has to draw it: the app imports KML, KMZ, shapefiles, GeoJSON, GPX, CSV and WKT, and it even reads GeoPDF: with a georeference it paints the plan over the map and pulls parcel rings out of the vector layer.

Every parcel keeps its record: planting history, soil measurements and forage assessments. It's the digital version of the monitoring the Hacienda keeps today on paper and Excel, what their reports call PRIAS.

The button that matters is the analysis. The app asks NASA POWER's climatology for that spot on the planet (rainfall, temperature, root-zone moisture) and returns three things: the top 3 of what to plant after what was there, ranked by nitrogen contribution, previous family, pH and rainfall; the forage planting window, meaning the months with enough rain per the climatology; and a PDF report built to be sent over WhatsApp, the channel the Hacienda actually uses.

The catalog already carries their species: 19 grasses and legumes from the Patiño pasture mix. And the whole setup, site, app, emails and PDF, speaks English and Spanish: the ES/EN switch sticks, and backend strings travel with `lang`.

First real verdict, on Hacienda coordinates in November: the planting window runs October through June, but POWER flags the year as dry, 27% less rain than average. The analysis already says uncomfortable things, which is what it's for.

Behind it sits an Express + MongoDB backend running the same code in two places: api.statetty.com for production and a standalone copy in the public repo, since the hackathon demands everything be open.

What's missing is known: the real soil sample arrives Monday, the Hacienda's own weather station gets wired in after that, and the forage harvest sweet spot, their declared pain number one, is still a formula to be written.
